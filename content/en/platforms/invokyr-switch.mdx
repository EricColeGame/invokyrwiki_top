## How Dice Rolls Shape Every Run

Dice are the defining system here. They are not a simple movement tool. Every roll is a potential escalation: creatures, traps, or a full environmental rewrite. That design forces teams to make decisions with incomplete information, which is precisely where the horror lives.

The goal is not to remove uncertainty, because uncertainty is the game. The goal is to shrink the cost of a bad outcome.

| Dice Situation | Main Risk | Recommended Response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Early exploration | Burning useful effects too soon | Preserve special dice until you understand the area |
| Low resources | One bad roll forces a retreat | Move as a group and identify fallback spots |
| New biome | Old routes no longer apply | Pause, observe hazards, then commit to a direction |
| Creature encounter | Panic splits the team | Call out positions and move as one unit |

### Save the Effect

Hold powerful dice until the team understands the current threat. Spending a strong effect on turn one usually means having nothing when the situation actually turns.

### Use With Purpose

Spend a special die when it solves a specific problem — opening a route, breaking a chase, or canceling a hazard you cannot otherwise handle.

### Share Information

Tell your teammates what the die does before you use it. A coordinated decision beats a rushed solo play almost every time.

One-use dice deserve special respect. They can flip a losing run into a survivable one, or they can introduce a fresh problem your group was not prepared for. Treat them as tactical tools tied to a specific problem, not as ordinary inventory to burn on curiosity.

> Dice discipline: never roll just because the next space is open. First decide what the team does if the roll summons a creature, a trap, or an unexpected biome.

## Co-op Survival Setup: Roles, Communication, and Timing

You do not need rigid character classes to benefit from structure. Community reports suggest that informal roles assigned by situation work better than fixed loadouts, because the environment keeps changing underneath you.

| Informal Role | Main Responsibility | Useful Habit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Scout | Read the immediate route and flag hazards | Report safe paths before the group moves |
| Anchor | Stay central and prevent separation | Remain a visible reference during panic |
| Resource Lead | Track one-use dice and emergency options | Announce when an effect is being reserved |
| Exit Watcher | Watch for retreat routes during encounters | Block pushes forward with no fallback |

A repeatable five-step loop keeps a four-player run from dissolving into chaos:

1. **Establish the group route.** Pick a direction and name the nearest regroup point before anything risky starts.
2. **Assign temporary roles.** Decide who scouts, who watches the rear, who tracks special dice, and who calls retreats. Roles can shift when the biome shifts.
3. **Announce the next roll.** Summarize team condition and likely consequences so everyone knows whether the priority is progress, escape, or information.
4. **React as a unit.** When a creature, trap, or biome change appears, execute one plan instead of four separate panic responses.
5. **Regroup before continuing.** Confirm positions and remaining resources before triggering another roll.

Communication rules that hold up under pressure:

- Use short callouts for locations, threats, and retreat directions.
- Confirm everyone is ready before any roll.
- Avoid splitting unless the environment forces it.
- Announce before spending a one-use die.
- If someone is isolated, regroup before you explore.
- When the situation is unclear, stop moving and reassess.
